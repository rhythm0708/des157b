What if AI was...

used in hiring?

For decades, hiring has been a labor-intensive, human-driven practice.

Recruiters sift through resumés, conduct interviews, and determine whether or not a candidate is suitable based on intuition and experience.

But AI is quickly moving beyond a supporting role in hiring.

It is becoming the driving force, transforming each stage of hiring into an automated, human-detatched process.

Think it won't affect you? A recent Gallup survey reveals that 93% of Fortune 500 companies have begun to integrate AI tools into their hiring practices.

For example,

This is a one-way interview.

In a one-way interview, you record yourself answering questions on a computer or phone, while a clock keeps track of time.

At some companies, they've started using AI metrics to assess candidate potential.

The AI tries to determine whether you're dependable, trustworthy, or capable, based on a facial attribute, something you're wearing, or something that's on your screen.

Doesn't that just sound... dystopian?

Not only is it dystopain, it's discriminatory. Studies have found that these methods have shown algorithmic bias towards the facial scans of white people.

A 2018 study showed that the images of black NBA players were interpreted to be more angry or contemptuous than those of white players.

What about scanning resumes?

Now HR reps are using AI tools to hone in on keywords and phrases in your resumé, eliminating you if you simply don't use a specific word.

But

It doesn't have to all be bad.

If companies communicate their AI policies in a transparent manner and carefully design processes to eliminate racial, gender, and historical biases, AI use in hiring could actually end up being more fair and effective.

Give that some thought.

What if AI was...

used in policing?

AI is changing the way police forces prevent, investigate, and solve crime.

Leveraging its pattern identification capabilities and speed at sorting and analyzing data, AI can potentially anticipate crime and reduce the amount of time between crime and conviction.

Surely that won't have ulterior consequences

...right?

Well one of the public's biggest concerns about AI in policing is that it can be used to justify or amplify existing discrimination against certain groups.

Since AI is trained on historical data, it risks being algorithmically biased to target and reinforce existing biases.

For instance, if crime data is collected from a minority ethnic community, the AI outcomes may lead to interventions that are targeted towards these communities disproportionately.

This method, which is called predictive policing, is shown to be able to reduce crimes by 30–40% and reduce emergency response times by 20–35%.

However, evidence is beginning to mount that suggests that predictive policing does not reduce crime, instead it only "worsen[s] the unequal treatment of Americans of color by law enforcement."

Predictive policing has been implemented by police departments across California, Washington, South Carolina, Alabama, Arizona, Tennessee, New York, and Illinois.

In LA, a system called LASER has been used to identify areas where gun violence is thought to be likely to occur.

However, it was shut down in 2019 after the LAPD inspector general released an audit finding inconsistencies in how individuals were identified and kept in the sytsem.

All in all, its a technology that people have tried and will likely continue to try in the future.

But does it serve the community? Can it be wielded in a way that actually reduces time and reduces human bias?

Only time can tell, with our voices guiding the direction of innovation.

What if AI was...

used for rehab?

There's a terrifying AI experiment currently going on where criminals can choose to experience minutes in AI prison instead of years in actual prison.

Think it doesn't sound so bad?

Well, this is how scientists describe it working.

Offenders are placed in high-tech pods fitted with headsets, and they're fed a stream of nonstop AI content.

Violent offenders may be forced to experience the crime from the victim's perspective.

Drug-related offenders may be fed memories that simulate the process of addiction and recovery.

In addition, the AI would also manipulate hormones and neutrotransmitters in real-time to force feelings of guilt, empathy, and remorse.

This concept is called Cognify, and it was designed by a Berlin-based filmmaker and science communicator who wants to overhaul the criminal justice system.

He argues that limitations of the current criminal justice system, like overcrowding and high recidivism rates, indicate that the current system isn't working. It's time for a change.

Do you agree with him?

As futuristic as this sounds, the technology behind this 'thought prison' is backed by real science.

Scientists have already been able to implant false memories into mice and transform their negative memories into positive ones.

In 2018, scientists transferred a memory from one marine snail to another, showing that memories can be artificially induced.

Plus with AI tools like OpenAI's text-to-video model Sora, creating these hyper-realistic simulations is increasingly plausible.

So

If you were forced to, would you undergo this simulation?

Or would you just serve your time in prison?

What if AI was...

a recycling bot?

AI is emerging as a powerful tool to help humans sort and recycle waste.

This is especially important as our waste generation continues to increase. In the U.S., the average citizen produces about 820kg of waste per year. Yet, only 24% of it is currently recycled.

A major problem in the recycling process is sortation. This is because incorrectly classified materials contaminate the recyclables and make them un-recyclable.

The current process relies on manual identification and precision on the line. The process has humans racing the clock to scan and move recyclables on fast-moving conveyor belts to ensure all waste ends up in the right place.

Not only is this process inefficient, it is also highly error-prone.

But not all hope is lost. AI advancements have offered new avenues towards addressing this problem.

For example, this is RecycleOS, a recycling program created by EverestLabs.

It is an AI algorithm paired with a computer vision system that allows it to sort objects with over 95% accuracy.

It is trained on a proprietary dataset of over 5 billion recyclable objects, and it can even provide data on the "shape, size, weight, material, packaging type, commodity value, and even brand information of every recyclable flowing through the plant," according to JD Ambati, founder and CEO of EverestLabs.

Accurately identifying recyclable materials helps reduce contamination rates and increase the purity of recyclables. For example, removing plastic bags that end up in the paper stream makes the end paper product more valuable.

Combined with collective practices of reducing, reusing, and recycling, this remarkable invention of AI recyclable-sorting systems could help bring us towards the US National Recycling Goal of 50% by 2030.

Now wouldn't that be neat.
